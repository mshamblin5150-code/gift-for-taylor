# The Maintainer is a hat, not a second account

ADR-0013 said the Maintainer "is the system's designer, not an ED Staff member" and has "no Staff-list or Schedule row". On 2026-09-24 reality contradicted that premise: the designer is a PRN dayshift RN who works this floor, belongs on the Schedule, and needs to stay there. `private.reject_maintainer_staff_link` refuses to bind the Maintainer identity to any Auth user holding a Staff account, so the premise had hardened into a rule demanding two logins on two email addresses for one person, permanently. This decision from #302 keeps ADR-0013's separation of authority and replaces the mechanism that enforced it.

The Maintainer is a **hat**, not an account. The binding in `private.maintainer_identity` stays at the database layer, where no Manager or Administrator can grant, remove or replace it, but it attaches to the Auth user the designer already has. Presence on the Staff list and the Schedule becomes a property of the Staff row, never of the Auth user; the same human holds a Staff row as a nurse and wears the Maintainer hat for repair. The old rule conflated two different facts — *has no domain authority* and *is not a person on the roster* — and only the first was ever the decision. `reject_maintainer_staff_link` and its sibling trigger on `staff_accounts` are removed, and the guarantee they were standing in for is restated below as behaviour the database still enforces.

The hat is off by default. Manager controls do not appear in the ordinary chrome, so the designer's everyday app remains a nurse's app; what exists only for repair lives on its own Maintainer surface. A repair that must happen in the ordinary interface — a Schedule cell the Manager cannot fix, a handover that refused — begins by **breaking the glass**: opening a Repair, which names a reason from a closed list and, for `Something else`, requires written detail. `current_staff_role()` grants Manager authority only while an open Repair is running, never merely because the Auth user holds the hat. The Repair is a row, not client state, because ADR-0018 gives SQL the rules it enforces; every audit record points back at the Repair that authorised it, and every such record names both the human and the hat, so no action is ever attributed to the Manager that the Maintainer took. Closing the Repair reseals the glass, and an hour's cap closes the one left open.

Each Repair raises a Notice to the Manager carrying its reason, which cannot be switched off. It waits in the app rather than reaching her phone, because the control is that she certainly reads it, not that it interrupts a shift. This is what keeps the hat from becoming how ordinary Manager work gets done after handover; ADR-0013 left that to intent, and intent is not a mechanism when the only Maintainer is the outgoing Manager.

Manager handover stays a single transfer, because this ED has one Manager and the word means that. Eligibility becomes a task done before the transfer rather than a refusal discovered during it: the successor's readiness is computed once in SQL, read by the picker and enforced by the same expression inside the transfer, and the control is offered only when what remains is named. The client-side `canTransferManagerTo` is deleted rather than widened — it disagreed with the transfer about `active`, could not see an Invite accepted but unconfirmed, and its second condition tested a column that is `NOT NULL`. Refusals that remain are defensive, and they carry refusal codes mapped to page wording as ADR-0017 and ADR-0018 require, across both Manager handover and Invite acceptance, because those are the two halves of one event and #302 exists because nobody could see which half failed.

We depart here from CIS Control 5.4, NCSC Cyber Essentials and ASD ISM-0445, which all require a dedicated second account for privileged work. Their threat is a privileged browser session meeting a phishing email, and their supporting controls are separate devices, jump servers and no mail on the privileged account — none of which a phone on a night shift provides, so two logins here would buy the form of that control and none of its substance. NIST AC-6(2) states the requirement the other way, in terms of "accounts **or roles**", and asks only that privilege not be ambient while doing ordinary work; a hat that is off until a Repair opens satisfies that better than a second login with standing authority. Microsoft's own defence of the two-account habit rests on it being needed "only occasionally", which is false for a Maintainer whose work is investigation and repair. Against that, no surveyed product models a working user as two logins: GitHub Enterprise carries authority above the roster on one account with an explicit act to step onto it, 7shifts makes schedulability a property of the job role rather than the account, and Windows UAC runs as the lesser token until consent is given. Nurses already know what breaking the glass means, and it already means reason-required, logged, and read by someone afterwards.

This supersedes ADR-0013's requirement that the Maintainer's account be distinct from any Staff account, and its claim that the Maintainer has permanent standing access to every Manager action; authority is now permanent but not ambient. Everything else in ADR-0013 stands, including that Taylor makes ED decisions, that Administrators cannot transfer Manager, and that a Maintainer change to Unit data carries a repair reason — now carried by the Repair itself. #304 tracks the hat and the Repair, #305 the Manager notice, #306 handover eligibility as a prior task, and #307 the refusal codes.
